> *This is from a communications perspective*

As a communications strategist who works with multiple organisations in different countries to come up with strategies to promote an international forum; I play the long-term game.

Plans for an event start a couple of years before the event even gets announced. But a lot of short-term tasks creep up too. Sometimes it’s rough to get things done on time, many times I can quickly get them done, there are times too that these tasks will be delegated to other team members where possible, and other times I’ve had to deprioritise the short-term things if they are not as urgent or as important. Preparing and following an annual communications and marketing strategy document helps me know what my priorities are, even when they change.

## The long-term game

On the comms end of things, before the main event or D-day(s), I also start working on a lot of things — speaking about branding, cultural contexts, tweaks, responsibilities, delegations, ticket sale targets, etc. a year in advance. I’ve been fortunate to work with seasoned professionals who care and understand how to build global communities and work with various stakeholders. They understand cultural nuances and know the respect to be accorded.

So a long while before our D-day (could be a major event or something else we’re launching), we’d have already mapped out our peak work days and slower seasons. For example*, May to July’ish* are summer breaks in most Western countries, so comms traffic tends to be slower because a portion of our target audience in the West would be on holidays. But we also recognise that in a good number of African and Asian countries, they don’t often observe the same seasons and work tides as their Western counterparts. Understanding the demographics is always useful.

Things pick up again between September to November. We know all this far ahead of time, so we plan accordingly. Although these *‘slower comms days’* revolve mostly around digital presences and we get slower email responses, we capitalize on those moments and work a lot more in those ‘quiet times’ to get much done, ahead of the big days and peak work season during the ember months.

Now you can see, that planning a lot of things a year ahead — peak moments, slow seasons, etc. — gives us a great head start with implementing the longer-term activities, which creates time to implement with laser focus and tackle short-term tasks when they come up.

One specific thing I do when creating the annual marketing and communications strategy is that I painstakingly map out a general overview of all social media and online activities to occur for an entire year. I have in that document, an outline **of what should happen every, single, week.** It is a working document so things can change, but I am that detailed. And yes it’s a pain in the ass to curate. It takes time, and I do this during the quieter season at the start of the year. But it is one of the best things ever that helps me keep my priorities straight and understand how to tackle urgent, short-term activities when they come up.

## The short-term game

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/1*8OC3jdbsZccgZQ0d4ckJeg.png)  
*-The Eisenhower Matrix*

For the short term, there is no magic formula but this will serve as a guide.

If it is urgent and important: **do it.**

If it is urgent but not important: **delegate it**

If it is not urgent but important: **schedule it**

**If it is not urgent and not important: eliminate it**

— The Eisenhower Matrix
