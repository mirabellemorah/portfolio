Building an international design fellowship is quite interesting. The [Grohwie Design Fellowship](https://www.grohwie.com/fellowship) is in its second year and its second cohort. **Now I’ve expanded fellows from 1 country to 7 countries represented.**

In the [first cohort](https://www.instagram.com/p/DXd67M8DHYk/?img_index=1) I welcomed 11 fellows. I wanted it to be small, where people feel heard and are able to connect and ask tons of questions. Also, there was a budget constraint due to giving fellows stipends. But for the second cohort I’ve decided to expand to 21 more fellows, in seven countries [(partner with us)](https://www.grohwie.com/fellowship).

> [Join me welcome the fellows of cohort 2!](https://www.grohwie.com/fellowship/fellows)

It comes with its challenges here and there but [I learned a lot of lessons from year one](https://medium.com/@mirabellemorah/ill-never-do-this-again-bootstrapping-an-international-design-fellowship-af2644b694e7).

### **Lessons learned**

The first lesson I learned from year one was

1.  **Have a team:** Hey you gotta have a team you’re working with and you have to know exactly what to assign people. Year 1 fellowship was 4 weeks; year two is eight weeks. Basically four weeks of learnings and me transferring all the business knowledge that I have to people, and the final weeks are just them working on practical design client briefs with real organisations.
2.  **Plan for the unexpected:** Interestingly, because cohort 2 is an expanded cohort, I wasn’t initially very certain what the reception would be like and how to encourage and motivate people to stay on. In Cohort 1, I had 100% finish rate, but context changes things so I had to stay on my toes to adapt to this extended model in cohort 2. So, I decided that I wanted to accept 21 fellows but I was going to send acceptance invites to about 31 people in case certain people declined due to time differences (USA/China/India). At the end of the day about 22 people ended up saying yes that they would love to be part of the fellowship. I also had a wait list just in case someone would drop off too. Nonetheless, 1 person emailed on the start of the fellowship day said, *hey I can’t do this again based on time zone.* That left me with 21 people*. 2 other people who were based in China said, hey this is like 2 a.m. for us; we love this fellowship, we want to be part of this, we’ll do our best to join even though it’s 2 a.m*. Based on lesson 1, I’d now contracted a community manager, someone who would be in charge of making sure that people stayed engaged, people were excited, and enough resources were being passed on to people.
3.  **Have systems: To** be part of the fellowship, one has to accept the invitation by a deadline or their spots would be taken (had over 100 applications for cohort 2 so yes, the wait list was long). A day before cohort 2 officially started, someone who’d been accepted but failed to accept by the deadline messaged to say they couldn’t meet the initial deadline and if their spots could be given to them. Unfortunately systems are systems, and honestly we were already at capacity and someone else had been reached out to from the wait list to take on their space.
4.  **Assign myself core tasks:** Oh I also need to add that, as you know, I’m a motion designer. I set core tasks for myself including:

a\. Being the lead facilitator.

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/1*znVEa6gSODe199to0CaZbw.gif)

b\. Animating and making all creative and marketing assets for the fellowship.

Those are just two rules I gave myself aside. But well … yeah, behind the scenes there were a lot more things I did like:

- Managing marketing, emailing tons of creative platforms to have them publish the opportunity on their platform.
- reviewing all the applicants
- going through everybody’s portfolio one by one
- reviewing social media pages
- creating forms
- reaching out to speakers
- paying for subscriptions
- developing the curriculum
- etc.

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1200/1*EOSDKu-zTQ0WjaI0JBV3ig.png)

I did all that mostly because I had experience from the past year and also because I used to manage global communications for an international organization where we used to have like 2,000 to 5,000 people in attendance.
