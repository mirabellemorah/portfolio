*This is an article on something about Javascript, theory, practice and innovation.*

I’m growing in my web development skills and for the past several weeks, I have been faithfully using Mimo.org to learn full-stack development while attending [Code First Girls](https://www.linkedin.com/company/code-first-girls/) (CFG) classes on web development Kickstarter every Tuesday.

JavaScript was a (*still is, but now a diminishing*) pain in my b\*\*t especially when I first came across it. I had to retake all the JS sections on Mimo twice. I just thought no! Give me my CSS! My brain hurt every time I saw *document.GetElementById().*

I wished the facilitator at CFG had explained more of what she was teaching, why the codes she was using were important, and more about their function. I wanted her to dwell more on the reasoning behind using certain snippets of code and the foundational “why” because I didn’t want to be writing code and not know its root. So Mimo was absolutely great in teaching me and supplementing for these extra details I needed.

## Why knowing "why" is important

Would you allow me to explain further? As a motion designer, understanding the functionalities and principles of design and animation (all foundations) are crucial and it differentiates an experienced designer from a beginner. Understanding basic principles can help you make simple things. But those simple things can feel and look explosively great. Why? Because you understand the roots and you’ve learned to break rules. That’s how innovation happens.

\*\*\*

So when it was time to design a website for our group assignment at CFG and one of the instructions was to use JavaScript on some buttons and elements, it wasn’t until I coded the actual website that I started figuring out why JS is important and what those functions I’ve been learning mean.

> ***That’s why theory and no practice sucks!***

One of the best paths to growth is to keep practicing whatever you learn.

Experiment on it like scientists do. In school, I was taught to always aim for 100% correct answers. And that anything below a certain high score meant I failed, so it made me regurgitate the theory I’d taken in just to get this 100%.

But that’s not really true in life.

When you take theory and put it into practice, you’ll start to understand that many good ideas started out as shitty ideas. You’ll learn that non-successful execution is still successful execution — not a failure. When you tweak your mind to see possibilities in impossibilities, you become unstoppable.

What people call your “non-success” (which is still a form of you successfully figuring out what doesn’t work) gives you a new theory that, oh, ‘doing this is not the best way to get from point A to B.’ Therefore, you can create a new hypothesis to mix things together, test it and try it out again to see what works.

Indeed the fear of failure in front of others holds us back from experimenting and finding our true calling (I didn’t say that though someone else did).

So cheers to consistent practice! And remember to give yourself grace. Take your growth in bits not in bulk (except you can) so you don’t burn out and get discouraged.

> "Intellect is not wisdom. Most modern intellect is the ability to memorize information. Intellects worship access to information rather than the application of the lessons. Without execution, information is useless. It turns information into wisdom others can learn from." - @Mastering_life\_

Oh and remember also to have high intentions and low attachments. That means giving things your best shot but not being attached to outcomes.

When you succeed, you celebrate and be happy and life continues. If you don’t get it right, sulk, be indifferent, do whatever it is that eases your stress BUT MOVE ON.

Low attachment means not taking things personally.
