> Break down every single task into smaller bits and you will discover your time thieves.

As a communications manager, you probably always have a million things that need to be done. From sending information to partners to coming up with content strategies or pulling down posts that should never have gone up.

You’ve probably also got a task management system. I use Notion, what do you use? And even with these project management tools, you still might find it hard to navigate through several expanding tasks.

For the top [10 communications challenges](https://www.swordandthescript.com/2022/08/communications-challenges/) comms managers faced in 2022

- 35% said they found it difficult to cut through the noise
- 31% said they had way too many priorities
- And 25% said they had ever-expanding duties.

We’re here for the 31% and 25%.

## Use these recommendations to deal with your ever-expanding list

1.  **Set up a communications plan or strategy to guide you through a quarter, or at best a year**. It should contain the overarching goals the organisation needs to achieve, KPIs you need to reach as well, content types you will be implementing, target audience, platforms you need to focus on, and key dates or events to mark. This helps you and serves as a guide stick towards your north star. It helps you keep an eye on your priorities in the long run.
2.  **Let everyone in the organisation have access to this strategy document.** Call a brief meeting, and get input from people on their tasks and how the comms team can also collaborate to make things work out. Have people input into this doc if need be, and have them aware of what the comms team is trying to achieve for the year. That way, everyone has a scope of your tasks and priorities.
3.  **When new tasks come up but your capacity is stretched, think thoroughly through the new tasks.** If they are not urgent and important, are not in alignment or do not take the organisation closer to the main goals that are set in the strategy document, consider communicating with the other party, and let them know that you have to deprioritise the task to another date, or delegate it to another person if capacity and time permits.
4.  Break down every single task into smaller bits. This will reveal your time thieves. If you don’t do this, you will either overestimate or underestimate the amount of time needed to complete given tasks. Saying you need to send out a newsletter by 20th May sounds fair and simple enough. Right?

However, you may need to do the following just to get that newsletter ready:

a\. Get information from different team members and stakeholders on information that needs to go out (1–3 days)

b\. Prepare a draft document with the newsletter copy (1–3 hours)

c\. Submit and wait for approval (2 hours — 3 days)

d\. Start designing the newsletter (ongoing)

e\. Pick out images that need to go out, as well as design any assets needed (2–4 hours)

f\. Design the newsletter itself, format sections, and optimize for mobile (1–7 hours — all depends on how fast you are, the amount of information that needs to go out and the format of the newsletter)

g\. Troubleshoot problems that were never expected (infinity hours)

h\. Proofread (10–30 minutes)

i\. Send final version for approval (1 hour to 3 days)

j\. Send the newsletter out! (2 seconds)

Some of these steps you may not have to go through if you work in smaller teams and have more ownership of the entire communications flow.

Always remember, whenever you have tasks on your list, break them down into subtasks. See what you can delegate to others, and see what you need to do yourself. This makes your work more efficient and work smarter. Breaking things down into sub-tasks helps you understand how much work you still need to put in to get an assignment to the finished state. And what not to take on.
